From burden to brilliance: the UX overhaul that transformed compliance.

The problem

Monday morning. The sales team refreshes their dashboard. Another high-potential merchant stuck in compliance review. Days pass, no progress. Frustration builds — another deal slips away.

Merchant onboarding at Payaza was bottlenecked by compliance review. Deals sat stalled for days, some merchants abandoned the process, others got buried in legal jargon and document requests, and support couldn’t keep up with the questions that followed.

Act 1

A mental approach

What if compliance didn’t feel like compliance?

Fixing compliance wasn’t about small tweaks — it needed a complete re-imagination of how businesses onboarded with Payaza. So we started with that one fundamental question.

Act 2

Understanding the problem

Instead of assuming the problem was just about speed, we took a step back. We mapped the entire onboarding journey, interviewed compliance officers, operations and the people on the front line — sales — and analysed merchant behaviour. The findings were clear.

How we investigated

Journey mapping

We traced the full onboarding path, end to end.

Stakeholder interviews

Compliance, operations and sales on the front line.

Behaviour analysis

Where and when merchants actually dropped off.

What we found out

01

Perceived overload

Merchants felt compliance requirements were simply too many.

02

Confusion, not resistance

The biggest drop-off came from unclear requirements, not pushback on compliance itself.

03

Manual bottlenecks

Reviews done by hand were the slowest step — and the most straightforward to automate.

Act 3

Risk as layers

A solution to perceived overload

Not every merchant presented the same level of risk. We didn’t have to be rigid about how compliance was structured — we could run a graduated model instead of a binary one.

The more documents a merchant provides to prove they’re a legitimate business, the lower the risk on our end — and the higher the threshold we can approve them for.

  1. High / Tier 3

    For high-value transactions and high-risk customers. Requires enhanced due diligence, ongoing monitoring and stricter controls.

    Outer layer

  2. Medium / Tier 2

    Covers higher-value transactions and moderate-risk customers. Requires additional verification and monitoring.

    Middle layer

  3. Basic / Tier 1

    Designed for low-value transactions and low-risk customers. These require minimal verification and monitoring.

    Inner layer
High / Tier 3Few transaction restrictions
Medium / Tier 2Average transactioners
Basic / Tier 1Low transactioners

Act 4

Designing the experience

Ask only for what a merchant’s tier needs — and always say why.

The tiered model turned compliance from a single gate into a path. Merchants pick where to start, see exactly what each level asks for, and can begin transacting at Basic while an upgrade is reviewed.

Key decisions

  1. Go live first, verify progressively

    Basic lets a merchant start trading on minimal information while higher tiers are reviewed. A missing document now limits what a merchant can do — it no longer stops them altogether.

  2. Plain language over legal language

    Research showed confusion, not resistance, was driving drop-off. Every request was rewritten to answer what it is, why we need it, and how to get it right first time.

  3. Automate the checks, keep people for judgement

    Manual review was the slowest step. Identity and registration lookups now run automatically, and officers see a queue of exceptions instead of every submission.

  4. One status for everyone

    The merchant, sales and compliance all see the same timeline. Nobody has to refresh a dashboard on Monday morning and hope.

What changed

Compliance stopped being a wall at the start of onboarding and became a path merchants move along.

Merchants start sooner

Basic-tier merchants can accept payments while their upgrade is reviewed, so a pending document no longer stalls a deal.

Review effort follows risk

Automatic checks clear routine cases, so compliance officers spend their time on the exceptions that matter.

Everyone sees the same status

Merchants, sales and compliance share one timeline, replacing the cycle of chasing updates.

Reflection

Compliance friction turned out to be mostly communication friction. Merchants weren’t against being verified — they were against not knowing what was needed, why, or how long it would take.

Treating risk as layers turned a gate into a path. The natural next step is to let merchants move up tiers as their transaction history builds trust, so the path rewards good behaviour on its own.
